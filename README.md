Research Analyst, School of Information Studies, Syracuse University
Rohan Ramesh Yadav

## About this task

In Task 6 I built synthetic media. I turned a coach advisory narrative about the Syracuse Women's Lacrosse 2024 season into a synthetic audio clip using ElevenLabs and a synthetic video using D-ID, both on free tiers, and then tried to detect my own output.

This task asks what that capability means. Phase A is the reasoning, starting from the artifact I actually built and working outward through hypotheticals of my own. Phase B is a policy for a specific organization that could be adopted as written.

The task does not ask for a survey of real deepfake incidents, so every scenario here is one I invented.

There is no synthetic media in this repository. It is written analysis and a written policy.

Task 6 repository: https://github.com/rohany395/Task_06_Deep_Fake

## The context I chose

The Office of International Recruitment and Admissions at Syracuse University.

I picked it because the audience is the least able to verify anything and has the most riding on being right. Prospective students abroad, aged roughly 16 to 25, deciding on something worth fifty to two hundred thousand dollars, most of whom will never see the campus before they enrol.

The other reason is the distribution. This content does not usually reach students on a Syracuse web page. It reaches them forwarded through WhatsApp and WeChat, stripped of its caption and its file name. Any policy that leans on labelling has already lost in that setting, which forced me to write something that does not.

A large part of international recruitment also runs through commissioned education agents who are paid per enrolment. They have the strongest incentive to exaggerate and the least exposure to consequences, and they are the part of this setting my policy reaches worst.

## What surprised me

**Nothing stopped me.** I expected friction somewhere in Task 6, maybe a consent prompt or a flag on the tone. No safety filter fired at any point in either tool. The path from a text file to a convincing talking head was completely clear.

**The video was not detected and the audio was.** I had assumed the opposite, because the video's flaws are the ones I can see. It did not matter. What a creator notices and what a detector reads turn out to be different things.

**The paywall works backwards.** I first read the subscription barrier on these tools as mildly protective. It is not. Anyone motivated enough to fabricate something pays for it and treats it as a cost of doing business. The person who should be checking is scrolling past a clip and will not pay anything. So the barrier is weak exactly where you want it strong.
